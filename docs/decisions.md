**The reconciliation rule used throughout, as amended by D-042 on 2026-08-31.**
Where the FRD and the prototype disagree, the FRD wins on everything the product
does: the flow, the states, the rules, the permissions, the calculations, the
sequencing, the fields a screen carries, and the acceptance criteria at FRD §20.
The prototype wins on how it looks: the component vocabulary, the layout, the
typography, the colour, the spacing and the iconography. The prototype is
guidance, not specification. Anything that rule cannot settle is in register 2
and is not resolved here.

#### DN-001 How long must the audit log, evidence and closed investigations be kept

**The gap, in plain English**
The platform keeps a permanent record of who did what, the proof behind every
duty, and the files behind every investigation. Everyone agrees none of it may
be quietly deleted. Nobody has said how long each must be kept, or whether a
closed investigation may ever be purged at all, and if so who is allowed to
authorise that.

**Example**
Deepa Iyer files the June profession tax return on 28 June 2026 and attaches the
treasury challan. In June 2033, seven years later, a tax officer asks to see it.
Does the platform still have the challan? Does it still have the log line saying
Deepa attached it and Anjali verified it? If the answer is no, the platform has
to say so before the first byte is written, because a retention rule cannot be
applied backwards to data that has already been deleted.

**Why it matters**
The retention floor is enforced from the first migration and cannot be shortened
afterwards, by anyone, including the administrator. Setting it too short deletes
evidence the firm will need. Setting it too long is a storage cost the customer
has not agreed to and, for personal data, a data-protection problem of its own.
This is the one decision in the list that has to be made before the first
production write.

**Blocks** [[SLICE-00]]

**Recommendation**
Set three floors and set them separately: the audit log kept for the longest
period any applicable regime requires plus one year, evidence kept for the
retention period of the duty it proves plus one year, and closed investigations
kept but never purged, with a documented review at ten years rather than an
automatic deletion. The reason for a floor plus one year is that a retention
period is usually counted from an event the platform does not control, and a
year of margin costs almost nothing while a shortfall is unrecoverable. If the
customer wants a purge path for investigations, make it a governed action
requiring two named people, logged like any other, and never available to the
administrator alone.

**Decision**
Accepted as recommended, in all three parts, and recorded as D-040. The floor is
laid in [[SLICE-00]] as a table holding one row per store with a minimum period in
years, loaded as reference data, with a database constraint that refuses any
update lowering a value and any delete. Nothing reads it yet, because no deletion
path exists anywhere in the platform; it lands now because it cannot be applied
backwards. The exact number of years for each regime is set at install from the
customer's own regulatory position, and the platform's part is that no path can
shorten whatever is set.

**Decided by and date**
The customer, 2026-08-30.

#### DN-002 What does signing in actually look like on day one

**The gap, in plain English**
Today there is no sign-in at all: a developer bar lets you choose who you are.
Everyone agrees that has to become real. Nobody has said whether the firm's
staff sign in through the company's existing sign-in system, or whether the
platform holds its own usernames and passwords, and whether a second factor is
required from the first day or added later.

**Example**
Farhan Ali opens the platform on a Monday morning to file the labour return.

itself holds, and then a code from his phone? The two answers need different
work, and the second one means the platform is now storing credentials, which
brings its own obligations.

**Why it matters**
Every access rule in the product hangs off knowing who is acting. The person
record already exists and deliberately holds no credentials, so the federated

for development only, and make impersonation a logged action that no ordinary
user can reach. If the customer has no such system, say so now, because a local
sign-in is a two-week piece of work with obligations attached and it needs to be
in the plan rather than discovered in it.

Accepted as recommended, and recorded as D-044. Sign-in federates to the customer's
existing identity system and the platform holds no credentials. The development

runs. Impersonation is not built as a support capability in this release, and FRD
§21.17 leaves that open for a later decision.

**Decided by and date**
The customer, 2026-09-01.

#### DN-003 Is "not the same person" enough, or must the checker sit in a different line

**The gap, in plain English**

#### DN-008 What turns a penalty into a severity

**The gap, in plain English**
The product's headline claim is that how serious a duty is comes from the actual
penalty for missing it, rather than from somebody's judgment. Every clause
carries its penalty tiers, quoted from the law. Nobody has said what turns those
amounts into Critical, High, Medium or Low.

**Example**
Section 6(3) of the profession tax act carries two penalties: two hundred rupees
if the return is filed within thirty days of the due date, and one thousand
rupees otherwise. Elsewhere, a company law provision carries one hundred rupees
per day with a two lakh rupee cap, and beyond that the personal disqualification
of officers. The first should plainly be Low and the third should plainly be
Critical, but there is no rule that produces those two answers and every answer
in between.

**Why it matters**
This severity flows into the risk attached to the clause, and from there into the
heat map, the domain aggregate and the appetite status the board reads. Getting
it wrong does not produce an error; it produces a board pack that is quietly
misleading. And every clause already ingested would need re-deriving if the bands
change after go-live.

**Blocks** [[SLICE-09]]

**Recommendation**
Three rules, in this order, and the first that matches wins. Any penalty that
reaches a named individual, disqualification, prosecution or imprisonment, is
Critical, whatever the amount. Any penalty that accrues per day without a cap is
High, because it is unbounded. Otherwise band on the maximum monetary exposure
against thresholds the administrator sets, defaulting to Low below one lakh
rupees, Medium to ten lakh, High above. The ordering matters more than the
numbers: an unbounded per-day charge is worse than a large fixed fine even when
today's arithmetic says otherwise, and a threat to a named officer outranks both.

**Decision**

**Decided by and date**

#### DN-009 How is one risk's residual score worked out

**The gap, in plain English**
Every risk carries two numbers: what it would cost if nothing were done about it,
and what it costs given the controls that are in place. The first is likelihood
times impact and everyone agrees. The second is called the residual, and the
specification defines how to average residuals across a domain, how to
reconstruct one at a past date, and how to project one forward, but never says
how a single risk's residual is arrived at in the first place.

**Example**
Meera's team rates the payroll deduction risk at likelihood 3 and impact 4, so
twelve out of twenty-five before controls. Three controls sit over it: two passed
their last test and one failed. What is the residual? Six? Eight? Does the failed
control count for nothing, or does a control that exists but failed still reduce
exposure below a world with no control at all? The prototype stores a number that
somebody typed, and the plan says nothing may be stored that can be computed.

**Why it matters**
The residual is the number on the heat map, the number in the domain aggregate,
the number compared against the board's appetite, and the number in the trend the
Risk Committee reads. Every one of those is meaningless until this is settled, and
settling it after the register has real data means re-deriving every risk.

**Blocks** [[SLICE-25]]

**Recommendation**
Derive it from the inherent score and the assurance actually held over the risk:
inherent, reduced by a percentage that comes from the mitigating controls whose
latest test is a clean Pass and is current, with a Partial counting half and a
Fail or a lapsed test counting nothing. Cap the reduction so residual can never
fall below one, and so no risk can be reduced to nothing by controls alone. This
has the property the product needs: a control that quietly stops working raises
the residual without anybody typing a number, which is exactly the failure the
platform exists to catch. It is close, though. The alternative is to let the
owner propose a residual through an assessment cycle and treat it as a
maker-checked judgment rather than a computation, which is what most risk
functions actually do. What would tip it is whether the customer's risk team
expects to argue with the number or to be told it.

**Decision**

#### DN-012 Who stands down when an allegation names a team rather than a person

**The gap, in plain English**
Anyone conflicted out of an investigation must be unable to open it, and the
platform is required to work that out rather than rely on people declaring it.
The rule is: anyone named in the allegation, and anyone who leads the department
the allegation points at. But a report at intake deliberately records a role or a
team rather than a name, so there is often nobody named to work from.

**Example**
A report arrives saying "the fund accounting team lead has been approving
invoices for a supplier he part-owns." Nobody is named. Fund accounting sits

person the accused reports to. Now change the wording to "someone in fund
accounting", and the same question has a different feel. Change it to "a senior
person in this firm", and the rule would recuse everybody.

**Why it matters**

#### DN-018 Which of the twenty two instruments in the library are real, and where does each document come from

**The gap, in plain English**

prototype so the screen had something to show. The platform's rule is that every

repository. The other eighteen do not, and for some of them it is not clear that
a document exists at all.

**Example**
The library shows "PFRDA: Clarification on single-issuer exposure and prudential
norms", dated 8 June 2026, sitting in the Needs decision group with one clause
against it. To create that record for real, somebody has to produce the PFRDA
circular of 8 June 2026. If PFRDA published no such circular, the platform would
be holding a piece of law that does not exist, with an owner, a clause and a
place in the register. The same question applies to five other instruments dated
2026, and a different question applies to ISO 37301, ISO 27001 and PCI DSS, which
are real but licensed and cannot be committed to a repository.

**Why it matters**
An invented act is worse than a missing one. It produces duties nobody owes,
chased on a ladder, appearing in a committee pack. It is also the one kind of
error the platform's own provenance rules are built to make impossible, so
allowing it once undermines every clause on every other screen. And the four

an instrument it may not store a copy of.

**Blocks** [[SLICE-06]] Part B. Part A, the screen itself, is unaffected

wants the library to look busy in the meantime, that is a demonstration need and
D-031 has already ruled the product is not used for demonstration.

**Decision**

`BR-EVD-07` intact because a hand-keyed verbatim extract is still verbatim.

document, or its licensed equivalent, is in hand. An instrument whose document

#### DN-019 What does the act status chip on a library row mean, now that provisions and clauses are two tiers

**The gap, in plain English**
Every row in the library carries a small chip reading Processing, In review or
Tracked, or nothing at all for a reference standard. In the prototype there was
one tier of clauses and the chip read straight off their statuses. D-014 split
that into two tiers: provisions, which are everything the extractor found, and
clauses, which are the ones a person promoted. The chip's three words have not
been redefined against the split.

**Example**
The Companies Act is ingested and produces 470 provisions. A person has promoted
four of them into clauses, saved two to controls, and left the other 466
untouched because they are machinery. Is that act Tracked, because everything
somebody decided about is decided? In review, because 466 provisions carry no
decision? Or Processing, because the two remaining clauses have no control
proposal on them yet? The three answers give the officer three different working
lists.

**Why it matters**
The chip decides which group a row falls into, and the groups are the whole point
of the screen. Get it wrong and the Needs decision group either holds every act
forever or holds nothing.

**Blocks** nothing. The reconciliation rule settles it, because the chip is
visible and the prototype's meaning wins. This entry exists so the translation is
confirmed rather than assumed

**Recommendation**
Read the chip off the duty bearing provisions only, never off the full provision
count, because a statute is mostly machinery and counting machinery as undecided
makes every act permanently In review. So: **Processing** where at least one
promoted clause has no proposal on it yet. **In review** where at least one
provision classified as a duty binding the firm carries no decision, or a clause
stands at Recommended or Specialist review. **Tracked** where every duty bearing
provision has been decided one way or the other. **No chip** where the instrument
holds no duty bearing provision at all. In the worked example above the Companies
Act reads In review, which is right: 466 provisions the extractor thought were
machinery is a claim nobody has checked.

**Decision**
The chip reports the instrument's onboarding lifecycle, not the disposition of any
one clause or obligation. Four values, in the customer's own words. Recorded as
D-034.

| Chip | What it means | How the build derives it |
|---|---|---|
| `Processing` | Intake and ingestion. The instrument has been ingested and is undergoing parsing, OCR extraction or assisted structuring into provisions and clauses | Extraction or classification has not completed for the instrument: at least one provision carries no `classifiedAt` |
| `In review` | Triage and applicability assessment. The structure stands, and compliance is triaging clauses, determining applicability and assigning ownership | Classification is complete, and at least one duty bearing provision carries no decision, or a promoted clause stands at Recommended or Specialist review |
| `Tracked` | Fully onboarded. Every underlying clause has completed its initial decision and the instrument is in steady-state monitoring | Every duty bearing provision is promoted, recorded not applicable, or with a specialist |
| `Superseded` | The instrument has been replaced or repealed, and its provisions become historical reference | The instrument's own `status` is Superseded or Repealed. This takes precedence over the other three |

Three readings are recorded so nobody has to guess later. **"Duty bearing"** means
`classification = Duty AND bindsUs = yes`, which is the same population
`isPromotable` already uses. **"Deferred"**, in the customer's wording of the
Tracked case, maps to the provision being with a specialist: there is no separate
deferred state in the model and none is added. **An instrument holding no duty
bearing provision** carries no chip at all and its column reads `Reference`, which
the prototype already does and the four values above do not cover.

`Superseded` is a fourth value the prototype does not have, so the chip's column
gains one word it did not carry. It overlaps deliberately with the legal status
ER-013 puts on line two of the row: the chip drives which group the row falls
into, and line two states the fact.

**Decided by and date**
The customer, 2026-08-30.

#### DN-023 Does the department gate apply per authority row or across all of them

**The gap, in plain English**
The platform decides who may do what from one table. Each row in it names an
action, a role, and sometimes a department the person must be in. Until now every
action's rows have been uniform: either all of them name a department, or none

**Example**
Priya Sharma is the Data Protection Officer. She holds Compliance Manager and sits

It is one line of logic in the one place every governed action passes through. Get
it wrong and the department half of `BR-AUT-02` silently stops applying to any
action that ever gains a second kind of permitted role, which over the plan's
seventy-odd actions is most of them. It is also the kind of defect that passes
every test written before the second row was added.

**Blocks** [[SLICE-06]] step A1, and it belongs to [[SLICE-01]] because it changes
the one authority check

#### DN-025 Does a sample person's audit trail block the sample purge

**The gap, in plain English**
The platform ships with a set of invented people so a new install has somebody to
sign in as, and one command removes them all again before the customer starts
real work. The command already refuses when one of those invented people owns a
real control, a real duty or a real task, because deleting them would leave those
records pointing at nobody. Nothing says what should happen when an invented
person has not owned anything but has **done** something: made a decision,
promoted a provision, verified a piece of evidence, or simply had their name
written into the platform's own log.

**Example**
Anjali Deshmukh is one of the seven invented people. During a fortnight of
setting the platform up, somebody signs in as Anjali and accepts PFRDA Act section

deletes Anjali and the clause keeps her name on it as an empty space, so the

line, and tells the firm to reassign the decision to a real person first.

The middle option destroys work. The first option is worse than it looks: the
actor's identity is part of what each audit entry is hashed with, so nulling it

person wrote. The next person to run `pnpm --filter api verify:audit` is told the

Refuse, and name what blocks it. It is what the purge already does for ownership,

provenance. The ten foreign keys that currently null a provenance field on delete

writes a delete by hand. The cost is that a firm which has done real work while
signed in as an invented person has to reassign that work before purging, and
that is the correct cost: the alternative is a platform that quietly forgets who
decided the firm was bound by a law.

**Decision**
Accepted as recommended, and recorded as D-041.

#### ER-001 The one calendar carries obligations only

**What the prototype does**
The Obligations screen carries a forward and current-month calendar of duty due
dates, filterable by regulator, status, frequency and owner. It is well built and
it is the screen a compliance officer would live in.

**What the FRD describes**
`BR-SCH-01` requires every deadline in the firm to appear on one calendar, and
§11.1 lists eleven kinds: obligation due dates and their cycles, committee
meetings, control test cadences, policy review dates, audit plan quarters,
campaign due dates, exception expiries, risk acceptance expiries, third-party
diligence dates, third-party assurance expiries, indicator refresh dates, and
live incident regulator clocks.

**Why the prototype's version is weaker**
A user must still assemble the firm's deadlines from several places, which is the
exact problem the rule exists to prevent. The month a firm has an audit quarter
starting, two assurance reports expiring and a committee meeting is the month its
compliance officer most needs one view, and the prototype's calendar shows none
of the four.

**Recommendation**
Build it. The calendar already exists and every one of the eleven sources has a
date field in this plan, so the work is a union query and a legend rather than a
new screen. Doing it inside [[SLICE-20]], where the calendar is already being wired,
costs perhaps three days. Retrofitting it after the cockpit is built means
touching a screen four modules have since come to depend on.

**Effort** medium: one union read, one legend, one filter per kind

**Decision**
Conformance, not an enhancement. `BR-SCH-01` reads "Every deadline in the firm, a
filing, a review, an incident-reporting window, an expiry, appears on **one**
calendar", and FRD §11.1 names the eleven kinds. The prototype carries one of the
eleven, so this is a gap against a stated rule rather than an improvement on a
working screen. Built in [[SLICE-20]] with no further approval.

One bound on it. The calendar carries every kind whose entity exists when
[[SLICE-20]] builds. Kinds whose entities land in later slices join the union as
those slices land, and [[SLICE-20]] states which of the eleven are live on the day
it closes rather than rendering a legend entry for a source that has no records.
Re-triaged under D-042, recorded as D-043.

#### ER-009 One seven-day warning window serves every kind of expiry

**What the prototype does**
`EXPIRY_WARNING_DAYS = 7` in `src/lib/exceptions.ts`, applied to every expiring
thing that reads through `expiryState()`.

**What the FRD describes**
`BR-ESC-07` sets three windows deliberately: seven days for a control exception,
thirty for a risk acceptance, and sixty for an independent assurance report. The
reason given is that an annual instrument needs a renewal in motion months before
a short-lived deviation does.

supplier's risk tier rises automatically, which is the platform correctly
reporting a failure it could have prevented by warning earlier.

Make the window a property of what is expiring rather than a constant, in
[[SLICE-24]], and make the three values configuration rather than code. The FRD
already lists them under what an administrator may change.

**Effort** small: one parameter, three configuration values

**Decision**
Conformance, not an enhancement. `BR-ESC-07` sets three windows by name: thirty
days for a risk acceptance, sixty for an independent assurance report, seven for a
control exception. The prototype applies one constant of seven to all three, so two
of the three rule's values are simply not implemented. Built in [[SLICE-24]], with
the window a property of what is expiring and the three values held as
configuration, which is where the FRD already puts them under what an administrator
may change. Re-triaged under D-042, recorded as D-043.

**Decided by and date**
The build under D-042, 2026-08-31.

#### ER-010 The obligation register shows a status but never whose desk the duty is on

**What the prototype does**
The register shows each duty's status, its owner, its due date and its regulator,
and the detail screen shows the maker and checker chain.

**What the FRD describes**
§5.28 requires the platform to answer "what is mine and what do I do next" and
§4.6 describes the Compliance Manager spending much of her day "chasing others
for the proof that something was done".

**Why the prototype's version is weaker**
A duty reading "In review" tells the compliance officer that somebody has
submitted it. It does not tell her who is sitting on it. She has to open each one
to find out, and there are two hundred and seventeen of them. The information is
already in the record: a cycle In review is waiting on its task's nominated
checker.

**Recommendation**
Add a derived "waiting on" column to the register in [[SLICE-16]], showing the person
the next action belongs to. It is one derivation over data the register already
loads, and it turns a status list into a chasing list, which is what the screen is
actually used for.

**Effort** small: one derived column

**Decision**
Stays an enhancement, and stays unbuilt. The two citations do not carry the
requirement. FRD §5.28 is the personal queue, and its step 3 shows each item with
"the record, the deadline, and the single action it needs", which is the queue
answering "what is mine". It does not require a waiting-on column on the obligation
register, which is a different screen answering a different question. FRD §4.6 is a
persona description, not a rule.

The case is good and the derivation is cheap. The FRD does not ask for it, so rule
11 applies and the customer decides. Re-triaged under D-042, recorded as D-043.

**Decided by and date**
Not decided. Awaiting the customer.

#### ER-011 The audit log's tamper-evidence is asserted on screen and checkable only from a command line

**What the prototype does**
The Settings audit log lists entries with actor, action, object and timestamp,
and the built backend hash-chains them properly. A script,
`pnpm --filter api verify:audit`, walks the chain and reports whether it holds.

**What the FRD describes**
`BR-AUD-02` requires the log to be tamper-evident, and §16 says the platform
should be able to demonstrate, on itself, the things it asks customers to
demonstrate. §4.9 makes the log readable by the second and third lines
specifically so they can assure the platform.

**Why the prototype's version is weaker**
An auditor sitting in front of the screen has no way to check the claim. The
verification exists and lives where only a developer can run it. A control that
can only be demonstrated by the people who built it is exactly the kind of
control an auditor discounts.

**Recommendation**
Put the chain verification on the audit log screen in [[SLICE-44]], as an action any
reader can run over a date range, reporting the range checked, the entry count and
whether every hash held. This is the single cheapest thing in this register that
makes the platform's own governance demonstrable, which is Requirement 24.

**Effort** small: one endpoint wrapping an existing script, one button, one result panel

**Decision**
Stays an enhancement, and stays unbuilt. The citations do not carry the requirement
the entry claims for them. `BR-AUD-02` requires the log to be append-only and
tamper-evident, and the hash chain with the database grants already achieves that.
Requirement 24 at FRD §20.1 demonstrates "a configuration change that is
maker-checked and logged, and an audit log that the administrator cannot edit", and
passes when the platform can pass on itself the test it applies to its customer.
Neither asks for on-screen chain verification.

The argument in the entry is still right: a control only its builders can
demonstrate is one an auditor discounts. That is an improvement the FRD does not
require, so rule 11 applies and the customer decides. Re-triaged under D-042,
recorded as D-043.

**Decided by and date**
Not decided. Awaiting the customer.

#### ER-012 The Source Library never marks an instrument as new or changed

**What the FRD implies**
FRD §5.1 step 1 makes the first step of the headline flow a system action:
surface new or changed instruments at the top of the Source Library, sorted so
items awaiting a decision float up. Half of that is built. Items awaiting a
decision do rise, because the Needs decision group is first. New and changed do
not, because nothing on the screen says which instruments those are.

**What the prototype does**
Rows sort by authority ascending within their group, then by the awaiting count.
An act registered this morning sits wherever its authority puts it in the
alphabet. There is no arrival date on the row, no marker, and no way to sort by
when something arrived. The only date on the row is the instrument's own date of
issue, labelled "updated", which is not when it reached the platform.

**Why the prototype's version is weaker**
The officer's first question on a Monday is what came in since Friday. The
library cannot answer it. They have to remember which acts they already looked
at, across an alphabetical list, which is exactly the memory the platform exists
to replace. It also means a superseding version of an act already held arrives
silently, next to its own predecessor, distinguishable only by reading the title.

**Recommendation**
Add two things: a marker on any instrument registered or re-ingested inside a
window the customer sets, and a fourth filter pill reading "Recent" that shows
them. Keep the group order and the within-group sort exactly as approved, so the
screen the client saw is unchanged for anyone not using the new pill. The
instrument already carries `createdAt`, so this is a display change rather than a
model change.

**Effort** small: one field on the read, one marker on the row, one pill

**Decision**
Approved, and built inside [[SLICE-06]]. Recorded as D-038. The group order and
the within-group sort stay exactly as approved, so the screen is unchanged for
anyone not using the new pill. How long "recent" means is DN-024, which is open
and does not block the build.

**Decided by and date**
The customer, 2026-08-30.

#### ER-013 The Source Library never shows an instrument's own legal status

**What the FRD implies**
FRD §7.1 gives an instrument four statuses: In force, Draft, Superseded and
Repealed. FRD §5.1 step 2 has the officer follow a supersession banner from a
current version back to the prior one. The status is load bearing: a duty
deriving from a repealed act is a duty nobody owes, and FRD §5.1 requires those
duties to be surfaced for review rather than retired automatically.

**What the prototype does**
The library row carries one chip, and it reports the clause pipeline, not the
law. `INST-PFRDA-INV-2025-MAR` carries status Superseded in the seed and its row
is indistinguishable from the version that superseded it, which sits directly
above it in the same group under the same authority. A Draft instrument, of which
the seed holds five, reads the same as one in force.

**Why the prototype's version is weaker**
The screen's job is to tell an officer which law binds the firm. It currently
shows which clauses have been dealt with, which is a different question. An
officer can promote a provision out of a superseded act without the screen ever
suggesting they should not, and the refusal that would stop them does not exist
either.

**Recommendation**
Add the instrument's status beside its type on line two of the row, as text
rather than a second chip, and suppress it where the status is In force so the
common case looks exactly as approved. Then a Draft, Superseded or Repealed act
announces itself and everything else is unchanged. Colour is not needed and
ADR-011 argues against it here: the status is a fact about the record, not a
signal about its health.

**Effort** small: one field already on the read, one span on the row

**Decision**
Approved, and built inside [[SLICE-06]]. Recorded as D-039. Status appears as text
beside the type on line two, suppressed where the status is In force, so the
common case looks exactly as approved. No colour, per ADR-011: the status is a
fact about the record, not a signal about its health.

It overlaps deliberately with D-034, which gives the act status chip a fourth
value of `Superseded`. The chip drives which group the row falls into; line two
states the fact. A superseded instrument therefore says so twice, in two
registers, and that is accepted rather than tidied away.

**Decided by and date**
The customer, 2026-08-30.
